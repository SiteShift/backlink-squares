Backlinks not showing in Google Search Console do not automatically mean a placement has disappeared or Google has ignored your website. Start by separating two questions: **does the link exist now, and does this particular report show it?** They require different evidence.

Google describes the Links report as a sample, combines some duplicate links and groups destination pages by canonical URL. Individual tables have limits. Treat it as useful evidence about your link profile, not a receipt for every placement. [Google’s Links report documentation](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9049606?hl=en)

This troubleshooting workflow helps you decide whether to wait, correct your own URL handling, or contact the publisher. It belongs alongside a broader [backlink audit](/backlink-audit); it is not a reason to start deleting links or submitting a disavow file.

## Start with this decision table

| What you observe | What it establishes | Next useful check |
|---|---|---|
| The link appears on a readable source page | A placement exists for the browser you used | Inspect its actual destination and attributes |
| The link exists but GSC omits it | The report and your observation differ | Check the property, destination grouping and export scope |
| The source returns 404 or 410 | That requested source URL is missing | Look for a moved article before contacting the publisher |
| Your scanner receives 403 or times out | The scanner could not inspect the page | Check manually; do not mark the link removed |
| The link leads to your old URL | The publisher still uses the old address | Test the full redirect to the intended replacement |
| Several tools show different counts | Their datasets or scopes differ | Compare exact source URLs, not just headline totals |

Keep the source URL, intended target and date of your observation together. A screenshot of a domain-level metric does not explain what happened to a particular link.

## 1. Verify the source rather than the total

Open the exact page where you expected the link. A publisher’s homepage is not a substitute for the article URL. Search within the page for your brand, the linked phrase and your domain. Check whether the link is inside the article, an author profile, a comments section or a navigation element.

Use the [live backlink checker](/tools/live-backlink-checker) with that source URL and your domain. Record the result as one of three states:

- **Found:** save the source, destination, anchor and attributes.
- **Not found in raw HTML:** inspect the rendered page before concluding removal.
- **Could not verify:** keep the result unresolved and record the access error.

A browser can display content that depends on JavaScript, a session or interaction. A simple HTML fetch does not reproduce all of those conditions. If the evidence conflicts, retain both observations rather than overwriting a previous successful check with “lost”. Our [placement verification guide](/blog/verify-backlink-placement) explains that distinction in more detail.

## 2. Confirm the property and destination

Write down the link’s destination exactly, including the hostname and path. Then compare it with the Search Console property you are using. A property limited to one URL prefix can have a different scope from a domain property.

Next, follow the destination in a browser. Has it moved from `www` to the root domain? Does an old product page redirect to a replacement? Is a canonical declaration consolidating a parameterised version into another URL? These are reasons to inspect the preferred destination, not assume the publisher sent you the wrong link.

For your own pages, compare the declared canonical with the URL Google selected using URL Inspection. Do not use a canonical tag on your site as proof of what Google decided. If there is a real mismatch, investigate the content, redirects and internal links together before changing one signal in isolation.

**Worked example:** a source links to `/guide?ref=partner`, while your preferred page is `/guide`. Looking only for the parameterised address in a report can miss the useful comparison. Save both the observed URL and the preferred destination in your worksheet. This example illustrates URL handling; it is not a claim about a specific site’s indexing.

## 3. Compare the right report and export

A report of linking websites answers a different question from a list of individual source pages. One publisher may have several pages linking to you. Conversely, repeated links can be grouped or deduplicated. Comparing those totals directly creates an apparent problem that may be a reporting difference.

Use the export appropriate to the question. Google offers “Latest links” and “More sample links” exports in addition to individual-table exports. These are still bounded datasets, not a complete public backlink index. [Export options and report limits](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9049606?hl=en)

For a repeatable comparison, save:

| Field | Why you need it |
|---|---|
| Property and report name | Prevents mixing incompatible scopes |
| Export date | Makes the observation reproducible |
| Exact source URL | Lets you check the placement itself |
| Observed target and final target | Separates publisher changes from your redirects |
| Current check status | Distinguishes missing from inaccessible |
| Next action and owner | Turns the export into a working queue |

The [GSC links analyser](/tools/gsc-links-analyzer) can summarise an English Top linking sites export. It cannot turn that sample into a complete history or prove that omitted links were lost.

## 4. Keep indexing and link reporting separate

There are at least three different objects to investigate: the source page, the link on it, and your destination page. A problem with one does not establish the status of the others.

You can inspect your own verified destination in Search Console. You usually cannot inspect a third-party publisher’s URL there unless you have access to that property. Ask the publisher about a genuine access or indexing concern only after you have a concrete URL and symptom to discuss.

Avoid treating a `site:` search, a cached screenshot or the output of a third-party checker as definitive indexing evidence. Equally, a source being indexable does not guarantee Google will list a particular link in your Links report or give it ranking weight. The [Page indexing report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7440203?hl=en) is the place to investigate the status of your own pages.

## 5. Escalate only the issue you can demonstrate

A useful message is specific: “The resource link on this page points to our retired URL, which now returns 404. Here is the relevant replacement.” It gives the editor something they can verify and fix.

“Your backlink is missing from my SEO dashboard” gives them much less to work with. Do not ask an editor to remove a sponsored or nofollow attribute simply to make a report look better. Those attributes have their own purpose and are not a GSC inclusion switch.

If the placement works, the target resolves correctly and you have no other symptom, schedule a later check instead of repeatedly changing URLs. There is no dependable publication-to-report deadline to promise. Record what changed between checks; do not invent a discovery date from the day you first noticed the link.
