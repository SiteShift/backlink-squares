A **backlink database** can mean two different things: an index of links that already exist, or a prospecting list of websites you could approach. Understand which one you are buying before comparing prices. An existing-link index helps you analyze a site. A prospect list helps you decide where to investigate a possible submission or pitch.

The [Complete Backlink Database Bundle](/bundle) is the second type. It contains 276 opportunities in a CSV, not a live index of your backlinks and not 276 guaranteed placements. This guide explains how to evaluate that kind of file and use it responsibly.

## Check the deliverable before the headline count

Ask to see actual sample rows. A useful file should make your next action easier: identify the publisher, explain the category, show a submission route and provide enough context to judge whether the opportunity applies to you.

A very large list can still be poor value if it is mostly duplicate domains, dead submission forms or sites unrelated to your business. A smaller shortlist may be more useful if it saves real qualification work. Count suitable opportunities after filtering, not just rows in a spreadsheet.

## Start with a clear campaign objective

Choose what you want a placement to accomplish. A software review profile may help potential buyers compare products. A local association page may help nearby customers discover a business. A resource-page mention may send readers to a genuinely useful guide. Those are different objectives and require different evidence of fit.

Write one sentence describing your audience and one describing the page you want them to visit. For example: “We serve independent design agencies, and our landing page explains project budgeting.” That makes it easier to reject a generic directory with no relevant audience, even if its domain metric looks attractive.

Do not treat every available form as an invitation to submit. Many platforms require a real product, eligible geography, active community participation or a particular kind of content. If you cannot meet the requirements honestly, remove the row from the active shortlist.

## Qualify each prospect in four passes

### 1. Audience relevance

Visit the site and inspect current content. Can you explain why its readers would want your product or resource? Look beyond a broad category label. A podcast directory, for example, is normally useful only if you have an eligible podcast to list.

Use the [relevance guide](/backlink-quality/relevance-factors) to think about subject matter, audience and page context. A domain-wide score cannot replace those checks.

### 2. Editorial and technical quality

Open existing listings or articles. Are they useful, readable and accessible? Is the site maintained? Can a normal visitor reach the page through navigation? Do pages appear to exist only to sell links? Record what you observed rather than assigning a vague “high quality” label.

A tool score can help you order the review queue, but it should not decide the outcome. Read the [prospect qualification checklist](/resources/link-prospect-qualification-checklist) for additional evidence to record.

### 3. Costs and requirements

Check the current submission rules and distinguish required fees from optional promotion. Budget for third-party costs separately from the research file. Check whether a listing renews automatically, whether a membership is required, and what material you need to supply.

Do not assume a database's recorded cost is still current. Publisher pricing and submission policies can change without the file changing.

### 4. Link attributes and measurement

Review examples of published pages, but remember that your eventual placement may differ. Paid links should be qualified as sponsored or nofollow. A nofollow link can still be useful for readers; an unqualified link does not guarantee ranking improvement.

Decide how you will assess the outcome: accepted listing, useful referral visits, relevant leads or another business measure. Keep these separate from domain-score changes and raw submission counts.

## Build a manageable shortlist

Start with a batch small enough to review carefully. Label each row “qualified,” “needs research” or “not suitable,” and write the reason. This makes future reviews faster and prevents a colleague from repeating work on a rejected prospect.

Add an owner, next action and review date. Use the [free prospect tracker](/templates) or adapt the [prospecting spreadsheet guide](/resources/prospecting-spreadsheet). Keep a master copy of the original file so your campaign notes do not overwrite source data.

For an illustrative example, suppose you review 30 rows and find eight that fit your audience, five that need more research and 17 that do not apply. That is a useful qualification outcome, not a disappointing conversion rate. The point is to avoid spending time on unsuitable submissions. These numbers are a teaching example, not BacklinkGrid customer results.

## Prepare useful submissions

Follow the publisher's instructions. Use a concise description, accurate category, working URL and any required screenshots or evidence. If the opportunity requires a pitch, explain the specific value for its readers rather than requesting a backlink as the main benefit.

The [outreach templates](/resources/outreach-email-templates) can help structure a message, but should be personalized. Do not automate mass submissions or send repeated messages after an opt-out. The [outreach prospecting guide](/outreach/prospecting) connects qualification to a sensible contact process.

## Record the actual result

A submitted form is not a published link. Save the final URL when accepted, verify the destination and attributes, and note any cost or renewal requirement. If a page changes later, investigate before contacting the publisher.

Review your campaign against the original objective. A useful listing may support discovery without a measurable ranking change. A domain-score increase may have no corresponding revenue impact. Report those facts separately rather than attributing every change to the database.
